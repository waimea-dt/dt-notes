- 1950: Magnetic drum memory

	Atlas magnetic drum memory stored information on a rotating, magnetically coated cylinder. It could store about 50 kB and offered a faster alternative to punched cards and paper tape.

	<captioned>

	![](./_assets/1950s/magdrum.jpg)

	Atlas magnetic drum memory

	</captioned>

- 1950: UNIVAC magnetic tape

	UNIVAC used magnetic tape as a commercial storage medium. Each long metal tape could hold around 3 MB, making it practical to keep much larger datasets than punched cards could hold.

	<captioned>

	![](./_assets/1950s/magtape.jpg)

	Magnetic tape storage

	</captioned>

- 1950s: Commercial computers arrive

	Computers changed from one-off research machines into products sold to organisations. The RCA 501 and UNIVAC were examples of systems marketed for general business and administrative work. By 1953, there were an estimated 100 computers in the world.

	<captioned>

	![](./_assets/1950s/rca.jpg)

	A promotional booklet for the RCA 501

	</captioned>

- 1951: Magnetic-core memory

	Magnetic-core memory stored bits in tiny doughnut-shaped rings threaded into a mesh of wires. It was reliable, compact and remained an important computer memory technology for decades.

	<captioned>

	![](./_assets/1950s/magcore.jpg)

	Magnetic-core memory

	</captioned>

- 1953: FLOW-MATIC

	Grace Hopper developed FLOW-MATIC, one of the first high-level programming languages. She worked for the US Navy and later became Admiral Grace Hopper.

	<captioned>

	![](./_assets/1950s/hopper.jpg)

	Grace Hopper

	</captioned>

- 1954: FORTRAN

	IBM programmers led by John Backus developed FORTRAN, a language designed for mathematical and scientific work. Its descendants remain important in high-performance computing and engineering.


- 1956: The RAMAC 305 disk drive

	IBM's RAMAC 305 was the world's first commercial hard-disk drive. Its 50 magnetic disks stored about 5 MB and provided much faster access to large datasets than tape or punched cards.

	<captioned>

	![](./_assets/1950s/ramac.jpg)

	The IBM RAMAC 305

	</captioned>

- 1956: Keyboard input

	Researchers began experimenting with entering data directly through a keyboard instead of using punched cards or paper tape. Devices such as the Flexowriter helped establish the keyboard as a practical computer input device.

	<captioned>

	![](./_assets/1950s/flexowriter.jpg)

	A Flexowriter

	</captioned>

- 1958: Integrated circuits

	Jack Kilby and Robert Noyce independently helped develop the integrated circuit: multiple electronic components manufactured together on a single chip. Integrated circuits made computers smaller, faster and more reliable.

	<captioned>

	![](./_assets/1950s/ic.jpg)

	An early integrated circuit

	</captioned>


- 1958: LISP

	John McCarthy created LISP at MIT. Its list-processing model made it well suited to symbolic reasoning and artificial intelligence research.


- 1959: COBOL

	FLOW-MATIC's ideas helped shape COBOL, a business language whose modern descendants are still used in important systems. COBOL is still used by the systems at the heart of many banks.

	<captioned>

	![](./_assets/1950s/cobol.jpg)

	COBOL, a high-level language

	</captioned>
