The 1950s transitioned computing from experimental wartime projects into **commercial enterprise systems**, driven by the invention of the **transistor** which began replacing fragile vacuum tubes.

This decade saw the debut of UNIVAC I, the first major US **commercial computer**, and the birth of **high-level programming languages** like FORTRAN, which allowed humans to write code using English-like commands instead of binary.

<timeline>

- 1950s: Commercial Computers Arrive

	Computers changed from one-off research machines into **products** sold to organisations. The RCA 501 and UNIVAC were examples of systems marketed for general business and administrative work. By 1953, there were an estimated **100 computers in the world**.

	<captioned>

	![](./_assets/1950s/rca.jpg)

	A promotional booklet for the RCA 501

	</captioned>

- 1950: Magnetic Drum Memory

	The ATLAS-1 computer used ERA **magnetic drum memory** to store data. Information was written to and read from a rotating, magnetically coated cylinder. It could store about **50kB** and offered a faster alternative to punched cards and paper tape.

	<captioned>

	![](./_assets/1950s/magdrum.jpg)

	Atlas magnetic drum memory

	</captioned>

- 1951: UNIVAC I

    The **UNIVAC (Universal Automatic Computer) I** was the first general-purpose electronic digital computer for **business applications** produced in the US. The first UNIVAC was acquired by the United States Census Bureau on March 31, 1951. A UNIVAC machine famously predict the result of the 1952 presidential election from a sample of a mere 5.5% of the voter turnout.

	<captioned>

	![](./_assets/1950s/univac.jpg)

	The UNIVAC I

	</captioned>

- 1951: Magnetic Tape

	UNIVAC used **magnetic tape** as a commercial storage medium. Each long metal tape could hold around **3MB**, making it practical to keep much larger datasets than punched cards could hold.

	<captioned>

	![](./_assets/1950s/magtape.jpg)

	Magnetic tape storage

	</captioned>

- 1951: Magnetic-Core Memory

	**Magnetic-core memory** stored bits in tiny doughnut-shaped magnets threaded into a mesh of wires. It was **reliable** and **compact**, and remained an important computer memory technology for decades, including being used in the Apollo space programme.

	<captioned>

	![](./_assets/1950s/magcore.jpg)

	Magnetic-core memory

	</captioned>

- 1953: FLOW-MATIC Programming Language

	**Grace Hopper** developed **FLOW-MATIC**, one of the first **high-level programming languages**. She worked for the US Navy and later became Rear Admiral Grace Hopper having spent years leading and developing some of the most important software systems of the time.

	<captioned>

	![](./_assets/1950s/hopper.jpg)

	Grace Hopper

	</captioned>

- 1954: FORTRAN

	**IBM** programmers led by **John Backus** developed **FORTRAN**, a language designed for **mathematical, scientific and engineering work**.

    FORTRAN stands for **FORmula TRANslator**, and modern versions are used today in weather prediction, finite element analysis, computational fluid dynamics, plasma physics, and many other fields. It is also used for programs that benchmark and rank the world's fastest supercomputers.

	<captioned>

	![](./_assets/1950s/fortran.jpg)

	Fortran language document

	</captioned>


- 1956: RAMAC 305 Disk Drive

	**IBM**'s **RAMAC 305** was the world's **first commercial hard-disk drive**. Its 50 magnetic disks stored about **5MB** and provided much faster access to large datasets than tape or punched cards. It was leased to customers, costing US$3,200 per month (equivalent to US$38,000 per month today).

	<captioned>

	![](./_assets/1950s/ramac.jpg)

	The IBM RAMAC 305

	</captioned>

- 1956: Keyboard Input

	Researchers began experimenting with **entering data directly through a keyboard** instead of using punched cards or paper tape. Devices such as the **Flexowriter** helped establish the keyboard as a practical computer input device.

	<captioned>

	![](./_assets/1950s/flexowriter.jpg)

	A Flexowriter

	</captioned>

- 1958: Integrated Circuits (ICs)

	**Jack Kilby** and **Robert Noyce** independently helped develop the **integrated circuit**: multiple electronic components manufactured together on a **single chip** of semiconductor material. Integrated circuits made computers smaller, faster and more reliable.

	<captioned>

	![](./_assets/1950s/ic.jpg)

	An early integrated circuit

	</captioned>


- 1958: LISP Programming Language

	**John McCarthy** created **LISP** at MIT. LISP stands for **LISt Processing**, reflecting its core focus on manipulating lists rather than numbers. McCarthy created the language while doing **research in artificial intelligence**, shortly after he coined the term "artificial intelligence" itself.

	<captioned>

	![](./_assets/1950s/mccarthy.jpg)

	John McCarthy in 1967

	</captioned>


- 1959: COBOL Programming Language

	FLOW-MATIC's ideas helped shape **COBOL**, a **business language** whose modern descendants are still used today in important systems such as **banking**. COBOL stands for **COmmon Business-Oriented Language**.

	<captioned>

	![](./_assets/1950s/cobol.jpg)

	COBOL, a high-level language

	</captioned>

</timeline>
